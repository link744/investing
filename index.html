In plain English

Every post on this blog is a snapshot. This one is a live experiment: for the next 90 days, an AI-run trading bot — not me making discretionary calls — manages a $1,000 virtual account, and I'll report what it actually does, with receipts.

The goals, stated up front so they can't move. One: beat 10% annualized on the seed — that's only ~$25 over 90 days, but the bot has to earn it through real entries and exits, not sit in cash. Two: protect the seed — if equity drops 5% from $1,000 the bot flattens and stands down for manual review; at −20% the experiment is over. Three: prove the paper-to-live path — the exact same code, account, and API would trade real money with a one-line host change, and that change happens only if the bar is met.

What it's allowed to buy is deliberately boring: 28 liquid US names, long-only, and only when the trend is up (price above its 50-day average and the 50-day above the 200-day). It ranks whatever qualifies by 21-day momentum, holds the top four in roughly equal ~$250 slots, and lets a broker-side 24/7 trailing stop — sized off ATR — get it out when a move dies. If the whole market breaks down (SPY under its 200-day), it halves deployed capital. No news watching, no mood swings, no second-guessing mid-day.

Where we are. Day 2. The bot went to work at the Sep 24 open and filled all four initial positions — AMD, PLTR, AAPL, NVDA — at $1,005.16 by the close, then added $1.13 on Sep 25 to finish at $1,006.29 (+0.63%), with a max drawdown of 0.33%. AMD already did the first "boring" thing worth noting: its trailing stop trimmed a sliver at the high instead of holding bag-holders' hope. That's the style: small, mechanical, repeatable — or it gets stopped out by the guardrails.

The honest caveat before you read too much into +0.63%: two days is not a result, and paper fills are optimistic (the bot logs every fill against the expected price to measure the bias). The verdict on 10% ARR needs the full 90 days — and then a longer look. I'll keep this post updated with the equity curve and a post at each milestone. The experiment, not the hype, is the story.

In plain English

Here's what this is saying: bonds are paying the most they have in 22 years right now. The 30-year Treasury is yielding about 5.4%, and the 10-year is back to a 2007-level. That's unusual and it's worth understanding before you decide anything.

Why are yields this high? Because the economy is hot and inflation reignited — CPI is at 3.4% after being near 2% earlier this year — so the Fed is hiking rates instead of cutting them. The market prices a 70% chance of another 25-basis-point hike in October. In plain terms: money is expensive right now, and it looks like it will stay expensive at least into December.

What does that mean for you? Two honest takes, and which one applies depends on your belief about where rates are headed.

Buying makes sense if: you want to lock in ~5% for 10–30 years with zero default risk (that's the 10- and 30-year case); you think the hikes are nearly over — if inflation rolls off and the Fed starts cutting, the bonds you bought at 5% will be worth more, not just pay you 5%; you need a risk-free floor on cash (T-bills at 4.2% genuinely compete with stocks now); you're retired and want income without equity risk; or you expect a later rate-cut cycle and want to be positioned when prices rise.

Hold off if: you think the Fed still has hikes left in it — in that case don't lock in long bonds yet, because long bonds lose value as yields keep climbing. Stick to the short end (T-bills, short T-bonds) and a ladder. Also: if oil and inflation keep surging, expect more hikes and short bonds let you reprice up as yields rise. And if you need both principal safety and growth, long bonds carry real price risk in this environment.

The balanced play the infographic points at: a barbell + ladder — short T-bills for the cash yield plus a modest long-Treasury position to lock the yield, plus dollar-cost-averaging in over time rather than one lump sum.

One framing note: this is not the 1981 bond bear market. Back then the Fed was fighting 14% inflation with a 20% funds rate. Today's problem is a hot economy and a reignited 3.4% inflation — the Fed is adding rates, not having just finished a brutal tightening cycle. The data says the same shape (yields spiking), a very different scale.

In plain English

A gauge that tells you when to buy and when to sell sounds like a free lunch. So we tested the exact rule — sell SPY to cash when the VIX is 15 or below, buy back when it’s 40 or above — against 35+ years of daily data: 10,252 trading days of SPY fund history and VIX closes from 1990 to 2026. The answer splits in two: the buy half works, the sell half doesn’t.

First the buy side — this is the part with high conviction. After a day when the VIX closed at 40 or above (true panic), the market was positive over the next six months 84% of the time, with a mean 6-month return of +17.1%. Only 5% of those panics were followed by a drop of more than 10%. In plain English: a panic day is, on average, the worst possible day to be out of the market.

Now the sell side — this is the trap. After a fresh VIX≤15 "calm" sell signal, the market was still positive over the next year 91% of the time. Why? Because a calm VIX marks the end of stress, not the beginning of the next crash. The gauge’s sell signal has historically been telling you "all clear" — and then you miss the next rally.

And the full all-in/all-out rule loses to just holding: over the sample, buy-and-hold turned $1 into 36.4× (9.24% a year), the best threshold combo 15.4×, and the literal 15/40 rule 6.0×. Even the best combo’s worst drawdown (−54.6%) is basically the same as buy-and-hold (−56.5%) — because even in cash 30–60% of the time, you’re still in for a big chunk of most declines.

We also tested the "use only the buy signal" version: buy at 40, then hold forever. It’s strictly worse than buy-and-hold, and the gap is exactly measurable — it’s the quiet compounding years you missed before the first panic. The max drawdown is identical in all three, because once you’re in, you eat 2008 and 2022 just like everyone else. The buy signal doesn’t beat buy-and-hold; it delays it — and the delay is exactly the years you give up.

How to actually use it (confidence: medium): when VIX hits 40, make sure you’re fully invested — that’s the edge. When VIX is 12 or lower, you can trim 20–30% for peace of mind. But don’t use it as a full exit signal — after that signal the market was positive 91% of the following year.

Honest caveats: the backtest assumes you can execute at the daily close — a VIX-40 intraday spike may not persist, and panic-market slippage would erode the edge. The 208 panic days cluster in the same bear markets, so they aren’t 208 independent observations. And the sample is 35+ years of one asset in one regime — a track record, not a guarantee.

In plain English

When the 10-year Treasury crossed 4.9% this month, the "1980s all over again" comparison started circulating again. It's a reflex, not an analysis — so this one pulled the actual Federal Reserve and FRED data for both eras and checked the comparison against what actually happened after each spike.

The short version: the direction is similar, the scale is not. What matches: in both eras the 10-year yield climbed out of a low, inflation re-accelerated above 3%, and the Fed tightened in response. In both eras the 10-year−2-year curve inverted as a genuine recession warning (the 1980 inversion was deep; ours dipped negative in 2024−25 and is only now re-steepening to +0.33%).

What does not match: the 1981 peak was the product of a 14.6% inflation crisis, a federal funds rate above 22%, and 11% unemployment. None of those three conditions exists today — inflation peaked at 9.0% in 2022 (about half the 1980 level), the funds rate is 3.63%, and unemployment is 4.1%. The "1980s" framing borrows the shape of 1981 and ignores its causes.

Here's the part most comparisons skip, because it doesn't fit the "yields are going to 20%" narrative cleanly: the 1981 peak was a ceiling, not a trajectory. In 1982 the Fed held rates near the top, then cut as the recession bit, and the 10-year fell from 15.8% to roughly 10.5%. Then, in 1983−84, the Fed re-tightened to keep disinflation from reversing, and the 10-year climbed back up — bond funds that bought the 1982 dip and held saw gains evaporate. The durable leg down in rates only came after the 1990−91 recession: the 10-year fell to the mid-6s and never returned to 15%.

What that means for you: if inflation stays above 3% and the Fed re-accelerates, the 10-year could push toward 5.5−6% before peaking — that's what happened after the 1980 and 1984 sub-peaks. If a real recession breaks the yield, it falls below 4% and stays down for a decade — that's the 1990−91 path. The consensus third scenario — yields stuck between 4.5−5.5% while the Fed fights persistent 2.5−3% inflation without a recession — is expert opinion, not something the data determines.

The lesson the data supports: spikes end as ceilings, and the durable move to low rates required a full recession. Same shape as 1981 — a very different scale.
