So, in plain English

The question in the headline was: 10%? 50%? The data-based answer is a range: −30% to −50%, with a point estimate of ≈ −40%. Ten percent would be the de-valuation-only case (1987 pattern); fifty percent is the deep end of the band, and it only appears when the AI capex cycle itself breaks — hyperscalers cut 2027 budgets, semi order books roll over, and a recession follows.

Why the number is as big as it is: the AI trade is not a small corner of the index. It is ≈ 41% of the S&P 500 — 34.5% in seven core names (NVDA, AAPL, MSFT, GOOGL+GOOG, AMZN, META, AVGO) plus 7.0% in ten major semis. At the March 2000 peak, the top 10 stocks were 27.6% of the S&P 500. Today's top 10 is 37.3–39.9% — 1.45× the dot-com peak concentration. When that 2000 bubble burst, the S&P 500 fell −49.1% and the Nasdaq −77.9% over 31 months. Replaying that drawdown at today's heavier weights gives the −38% (AI basket −70%) to −54.5% (2000 replay scaled) range in the chart.

One split dominates the outcome: whether AI earnings hold up. If the AI companies keep delivering and only the multiple gives back — the 1987 pattern, where the multiple compressed while fundamentals stayed intact — the same math says −15% to −25%. If instead the capex cycle breaks, it's −45% to −55% (2000 + 2008 combined).

What keeps this off the 1929 wall (−80%+): the S&P's forward P/E is 18.58–19.0× today, not a 2000-grade multiple, and there is no margin-equivalent leveraged bet stacked on top of the AI trade. And on timing: the 2000 analog took ~2.5–3 years from peak to trough — this is a slow-motion drawdown in every base-case scenario, not a single-day event.

What this means for you

If you hold the S&P 500 — say $10,000 in an S&P 500 fund — a burst at the point estimate takes it to ≈ $6,000 (−40%). In the earnings-hold case it's ≈ $7,500–$8,500; in the capex-break case ≈ $4,500–$5,500. That is the planning range to size your exposure against, because the risk is concentrated: ≈ 41% of what you own through the index is the same trade. If you're close to when you'll need this money, the honest question isn't "will it happen" — it's "can I hold through a −30 to −50% drawdown without selling?" If the answer is no, the fix is in allocation and time horizon, not in prediction. None of this is a prediction of when — or whether — it happens.

How I got these numbers

Every number in this post comes from the research leg of this batch (data as of the Oct 21, 2026 close: S&P 500 7,757.17, index market cap $71.35T) and is sourced at the bottom. The scenario math is a two-component weighted average: index move = 41.4% × (AI basket move) + 58.6% × (rest-of-index move). A sanity check: the same method applied to 2000's actual numbers (≈ 27.6% top basket × −75% + 72.4% × −40%) reproduces 2000's actual −49.1% S&P drawdown, so the method works on 2000 — and today's heavier basket is what pushes the number deeper.

  7. All index figures are closes, peak close to trough close. Intraday extremes (e.g., the S&P 500's 666.08 intraday low in March 2009) are not used.

  10. Evidence-based, not a guarantee. This is an expected range from historical analogs and current index weights — not a prediction, not a forecast of timing, and not investment advice.
Honest caveats

This is scenario arithmetic, not a forecast — the burst may never come, and if it comes it may not look like any of these scenarios. The numbers rest on a defined 17-name AI basket; a different list gives a different 41%. The "rest of the index" move is the free parameter — it's where "contained in the basket" and "recession" live, and it's the least certain input in the math. All figures are close-based (intraday extremes excluded), TSMC is excluded (it's not in the S&P 500), and no Fed policy path is assumed. None of it is a timing prediction; the 2000 analog only says that if it comes, it takes years, not days.
