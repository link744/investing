How the numbers work

Two layers, same as Parts 1 and 2:

  1. Long-run band (30–50%). Set by your runway to age 85: the shorter your remaining runway, the more bonds — 30% with 30+ years left, stepping up five points per five-year step, capped at 50%.
  2. The bond tent. In the sequence-risk red zone — about five years before to five years after your planned retirement date — the allocation ramps to a 55% peak at the retirement date, then tapers back to the band.
Assumptions

1. Data window: 47 annual total-return pairs, 1979–2025, US-only (S&P 500 TR + 10-yr US Treasury TR). 2. The bond asset is the 10-yr Treasury total return — a duration measure, not a retail bond fund. 3. Horizon at retirement = 85 − retirement age (a planning anchor, not a guarantee). 4. Constant allocation, no withdrawals; no taxes, fees, or income modeling. 5. The 2026 YTD figures are estimates and are not in the sample. 6. The band is evidence-based, not a guarantee — it reflects 47 years of data, not all possible futures. 7. The table does not model the separate 5–10% cash/T-bill income bucket, which is additive to the bond allocation, not a substitute for it.

In plain English

Your bond today is set by how much runway you have to 85. But your bond % is not a flat line — it’s a tent. In the ~10 years straddling your planned retirement date, you climb to a 55% peak because that’s when sequence risk is worst (a bad market right at or just after retirement is the one that can’t be made up), then you taper back toward the band as the danger zone passes. The table above is that tent, drawn age by age for your two numbers. The chart shows the same thing as a curve: flat band, tent peak at your retirement age, flat band.

Part 1 — re-published

Does "Age = Bond %" Still Hold? I Ran the Test on 47 Years of Data

Short version: no. At every age I tested, the classic rule — "put your age percent in bonds" — landed at or below a 60/40 portfolio, and almost always worse, including at the exact floor you're buying bonds to protect. Below is the chart, the data, and the version the data actually supports.

The question, honestly stated

You're planning retirement and you've heard the clean, memorable rule: bonds = your age. At 40 that's 60/40. At 50, 50/50. At 75, 75% bonds. It feels right — more life experience, more ballast. But it's a rule of thumb, and rules of thumb deserve to be tested against the data. I pulled 47 years of annual total returns (S&P 500 + 10-yr US Treasury, 1979–2025), ran a 20-year Monte Carlo at every bond split, and tested the rule age by age.

Your dollars after 20 years, by how much is in bonds. The blue line is the median (what you'll most likely get); the dashed line is your floor — the worst 5% of all 20-year outcomes, the scenarios you're buying bonds to survive. The green dashed line is the 60/40 benchmark. The red marker is the classic rule at age 75 (75% bonds): it lands at $4.71 — 34% below the benchmark — and its floor is worse, not better.

Two things to read out of that. One: the median falls every step of the way. Bonds are a drag on 20-year growth in this data — and the rule makes the drag longer, not shorter, as you age. Two — this is the part that should change your mind: the rule doesn't even buy a better floor at 75. The 25/75 worst-5% outcome is $2.58; the 60/40 worst-5% is $3.37. You get a lower median and a lower floor. That is the exact opposite of what you're buying bonds for.

Where the rule goes wrong

The rule was designed to cap drawdown, not optimize outcome. But the data says the drawdown you're protecting against is already small at 60/40 — 13.9% peak-to-trough in the worst 20-year window of the 98-year record. Going to 30/70 (the move a 70-year-old makes under the rule) makes that drawdown worse — 17.9% — while cutting your median 30%. You're paying growth for a safety cushion that the data says you didn't need to buy, and it's a smaller one than the one you already had.

What the data actually supports

The rule's instinct — more bonds near and at retirement — is real. The practitioners (Kitces, building on Pfau) call it the "bond tent": bond exposure should rise into retirement, peak at 50–60% at the retirement date, and then decline over the following 5–15 years as the sequence-risk "red zone" (5 years before to 5 years after) passes. That's the opposite shape from "age = bond %" — which keeps climbing forever.

Translated into a number for a casual retiree planning a ~20-year retirement in this dataset:

  • 30–50% bonds at retirement is the evidence-based band. If you're within 5 years of retiring, go to the top of that band (50%) and taper down after the first 5–10 years. That's the tent.
  • 5–10% cash / T-bills for your year-1 spending bucket. This is the piece that actually protected 2022-style scenarios in the data — not the bond slice. See below.
  • 60–90% stocks for the growth engine. 70/30 (with the 10% cash carved out of the bond bucket) is the single best single number for a 20-year retirement in this data.
The 2022 correction, and why it matters for the rule

2022 was the only year in 47 that stocks and 10-yr bonds both lost money — and it was the worst year for bonds in the full record (10-yr T-bond -17.8%; Bloomberg Agg -13.1%; S&P 500 -18.0%). The 60/40 was -16.9%, its second-worst year since 1976. That's the event the "age = bond %" rule is really trying to survive. But read the data: 2022–24 as a three-year start was actually net positive for every allocation above 50% stocks (60/40: +9.9%). The recovery fully made up for 2022. The real sequence-risk pattern is 2000–02 (dot-com burst), where 60/40 lost 10.6% in the first three years vs. -37.4% for 100% stocks — and that's where 40–60% bonds earn their keep. A once-in-47-years single-year event is not the same thing as a permanent 75% bond allocation.

And the timing, right now

There's a reason now is a good moment to be the bond-buyer. The 10-yr Treasury is at 5.22% (2026-10-08). A 7-year-duration intermediate ladder bought at that yield locks in roughly 5.2% nominal (~1.5–2% real) for the next decade. In 2022 the same ladder was losing ~18%. That's the lock-in benefit: same future rate shock, much less damage, because the carry is locked. And the cash bucket — 5–10% in T-bills — earns ~4% right now, so the 2022-style protection pays you to hold it.

Bottom line

"Age = bond %" is a clean rule and a wrong one. At every age it's tested, it lands at or below 60/40, and at the ages where it matters (65+) it cuts your median 25–38% with no floor benefit to show for it. What the data supports is a tent, not a ladder: 30–50% bonds at retirement, peaking near the date if you're within 5 years, tapering down after the red zone, plus a 5–10% cash/T-bill bucket that's the actual 2022 insurance — and, right now, at a 5.2% 10-yr yield, the bonds you're buying are paying you to hold them.

Honest caveats

This is 47 years of U.S. data (1979–2025) with 10-yr Treasuries as the "bond" — a pure-duration measure, not an aggregate bond fund (the Agg is what a retail bond fund actually holds; it was -13.1% in 2022 vs. -17.8% for the 10-yr). Constant allocation, no withdrawals — so this measures growth distribution, not the sequence-of-returns risk of actually spending down. 2026 YTD figures are estimates from FRED daily, not actuals. It's a strong argument, not a proof: the next 20 years are not the last 47.

Short version: the honest answer depends on exactly two things — how old you are today, and the age you plan to retire at. That table is below: two numbers in, one bond % out. The instinct from Part 1 is real — proximity to retirement is what dominates your current age — and the data turns that instinct into cells.

Try your own two numbers

Plug your own numbers into the Bond Tent Calculator (Part 3 of the series) — enter your age today and your planned retirement age, and get your recommended bond % at every age from today to 90: the long-run band, the ramp up to the 55% bond-tent peak, and the taper back down. The matrix above is one snapshot of that table, at the retirement date; the tool is the whole path.

How to read it

  • Columns set the long-run band, by your post-retirement runway — the years you expect to live after the retirement date, anchored at life expectancy 85. Retire at 55 → 30-yr runway → 30%; 60 → 25-yr → 35%; 65 → 20-yr → 40%; 70 → 15-yr → 45%; 75 → 10-yr → 50%. Longer runway → lower bond %; shorter runway → higher bond %.
  • The bold diagonal is the bond-tent peak, 55% — and it overrides the band whenever your planned retirement is within ~5 years. That's the "tent" from Part 1: bonds peak in the ~5 years before you retire, then taper back toward the band after. Read across that bold band and you'll notice the pattern: same years-to-retirement → same answer. Proximity to retirement dominates your current age.
  • The blanks (—) are where retirement age ≤ age today — you're already at or past your planned date.

How the table was built

Two layers, both anchored to Part 1's framework. Layer 1 — the long-run band (the columns): Part 1's evidence-based result is 30–50% bonds at retirement for a ~20-year retirement, so the 20-year anchor (retire at 65) sits at the band's midpoint, 40%. The same Monte Carlo as Part 1 (47 annual S&P TR / 10-yr T-bond pairs, 1979–2025, 200,000 bootstrap paths, seed 42, constant allocation, no withdrawals) shows that within that band the worst-5% floor is essentially flat while the median falls as bonds rise — so the band steps up only to buy stability as your post-retirement runway shortens, and the 20-yr cell sits at 40%. Layer 2 — the bond-tent peak (the rows): a sequence-risk check (first 3 years = the actual 2000–02 dot-com burst, then horizon-length bootstrap) is what justifies the 55% peak — at the 20-yr anchor it lifts the worst-5% floor ~18% (2.16 → 2.56 dollars per dollar) at only ~3% cost to the median (4.79 → 4.66), and it turns a 2000–02-style start from a loss (0.82×) into a net-positive first three years (1.01×). That trade is the tent, and it's why the within-5-years cells read 55% instead of the band.

Assumptions — every one of them

1. Data window / source: 47 annual total-return pairs, 1979–2025, US-only — S&P 500 (total return, incl. dividends) + 10-yr US Treasury (total return) — from the Part 1 research (Damodaran historical returns + FRED). No year before 1979 and nothing after 2025 is in the sample.
2. Bond asset = 10-yr US Treasury total return — a pure-duration measure, NOT the Bloomberg Aggregate or a retail bond fund. In 2022 the 10-yr T-bond was −17.83%; a retail Agg fund was −13.1%. The table's bond % is a portfolio weight against this asset, not against an Agg fund.
3. Constant allocation, no withdrawals. The growth Monte Carlo measures growth/distribution only, not drawdowns, income, or sequence-of-withdrawals. The separate sequence-risk table (first 3 years = actual 2000–02) is the supplement for drawdown/income risk.
4. Horizon at retirement = 85 − retirement age (planning life expectancy). 85 is a fixed planning anchor, not a guarantee; living materially longer or shorter than 85 changes the optimal horizon and hence the cell.
5. 2026 YTD figures are estimates, not actuals (S&P price + ~1.3% dividend; 10-yr bond = duration 7.5 × ΔY + average carry; T-bill ≈ avg Fed funds). The 2026 YTD row is not in the bootstrap sample — the Monte Carlo uses only the 47 full years 1979–2025.
6. No taxes, no fees/expenses, no income/spending modeling, no Social Security or pension offset — pure portfolio growth.
7. The band is evidence-based, not a proof or a guarantee. The Monte Carlo bootstraps 47 historical years, so it reflects the last ~47 years' joint stock/bond distribution, not all possible futures. 30–50% / 50–60% are ranges the data + practitioner literature support, not exact optima.
8. The % shown is for the retirement DATE. The actual glidepath rises to the tent peak in the ~5 years before the date and tapers back toward the band in the years after. The within-5-yr cells show the peak (55%); the other cells show the steady-state band target.
9. "Within ~5 years" trigger = 0 < retirement age − current age ≤ 5. Cells with retirement age ≤ current age are blank (not applicable).
10. Single diversified portfolio held to and beyond the retirement date; the table does not model rebalancing costs, tax-lot management, or the separate 5–10% cash/T-bill income bucket, which is additive to (not a substitute for) the bond allocation — that cash bucket is what actually protected 2022-style scenarios in the Part 1 data.
11. Single country, single duration: S&P 500 + 10-yr US T-bond. International diversification, other durations/maturities, TIPS, and real yields are out of scope.
12. The ~20-yr "retirement" framing and 85 life-expectancy anchor assume a standard retiree who lives to 85; the Part 1 30–50% band is stated for that ~20-yr case, and this matrix extrapolates it to 30-yr (retire 55) and 10-yr (retire 75) runways.

What this means for you

Plain English: your current age mostly tells you when you're getting to the table, not what the table says. If you're more than five years from your planned retirement, your bond % at the date is set by how long you expect to live after it — retire at 65 and plan for 40% bonds, retire at 70 and plan for 45%, retire at 55 and plan for 30% — and it barely changes what you do today except keep your stocks working. If you're within about five years, stop looking at the band: go to 55% bonds at the date (rising into it now, tapering back after), because that's where the data says a 2000–02-style start stops being a loss. And either way, the table's bond % is not a replacement for the 5–10% cash/T-bill bucket Part 1 talks about — that bucket is additive, and it's the piece that actually protected 2022-style scenarios.
