Does "Age = Bond %" Still Hold? I Ran the Test on 47 Years of Data

Short version: no. At every age I tested, the classic rule — "put your age percent in bonds" — landed at or below a 60/40 portfolio, and almost always worse, including at the exact floor you're buying bonds to protect. Below is the chart, the data, and the version the data actually supports.

The question, honestly stated

You're planning retirement and you've heard the clean, memorable rule: bonds = your age. At 40 that's 60/40. At 50, 50/50. At 75, 75% bonds. It feels right — more life experience, more ballast. But it's a rule of thumb, and rules of thumb deserve to be tested against the data. I pulled 47 years of annual total returns (S&P 500 + 10-yr US Treasury, 1979–2025), ran a 20-year Monte Carlo at every bond split, and tested the rule age by age.

Your dollars after 20 years, by how much is in bonds. The blue line is the median (what you'll most likely get); the dashed line is your floor — the worst 5% of all 20-year outcomes, the scenarios you're buying bonds to survive. The green dashed line is the 60/40 benchmark. The red marker is the classic rule at age 75 (75% bonds): it lands at $4.71 — 34% below the benchmark — and its floor is worse, not better.

Two things to read out of that. One: the median falls every step of the way. Bonds are a drag on 20-year growth in this data — and the rule makes the drag longer, not shorter, as you age. Two — this is the part that should change your mind: the rule doesn't even buy a better floor at 75. The 25/75 worst-5% outcome is $2.58; the 60/40 worst-5% is $3.37. You get a lower median and a lower floor. That is the exact opposite of what you're buying bonds for.

Where the rule goes wrong

The rule was designed to cap drawdown, not optimize outcome. But the data says the drawdown you're protecting against is already small at 60/40 — 13.9% peak-to-trough in the worst 20-year window of the 98-year record. Going to 30/70 (the move a 70-year-old makes under the rule) makes that drawdown worse — 17.9% — while cutting your median 30%. You're paying growth for a safety cushion that the data says you didn't need to buy, and it's a smaller one than the one you already had.

What the data actually supports

The rule's instinct — more bonds near and at retirement — is real. The practitioners (Kitces, building on Pfau) call it the "bond tent": bond exposure should rise into retirement, peak at 50–60% at the retirement date, and then decline over the following 5–15 years as the sequence-risk "red zone" (5 years before to 5 years after) passes. That's the opposite shape from "age = bond %" — which keeps climbing forever.

Translated into a number for a casual retiree planning a ~20-year retirement in this dataset:

  • 30–50% bonds at retirement is the evidence-based band. If you're within 5 years of retiring, go to the top of that band (50%) and taper down after the first 5–10 years. That's the tent.
  • 5–10% cash / T-bills for your year-1 spending bucket. This is the piece that actually protected 2022-style scenarios in the data — not the bond slice. See below.
  • 60–90% stocks for the growth engine. 70/30 (with the 10% cash carved out of the bond bucket) is the single best single number for a 20-year retirement in this data.
The 2022 correction, and why it matters for the rule

2022 was the only year in 47 that stocks and 10-yr bonds both lost money — and it was the worst year for bonds in the full record (10-yr T-bond -17.8%; Bloomberg Agg -13.1%; S&P 500 -18.0%). The 60/40 was -16.9%, its second-worst year since 1976. That's the event the "age = bond %" rule is really trying to survive. But read the data: 2022–24 as a three-year start was actually net positive for every allocation above 50% stocks (60/40: +9.9%). The recovery fully made up for 2022. The real sequence-risk pattern is 2000–02 (dot-com burst), where 60/40 lost 10.6% in the first three years vs. -37.4% for 100% stocks — and that's where 40–60% bonds earn their keep. A once-in-47-years single-year event is not the same thing as a permanent 75% bond allocation.

And the timing, right now

There's a reason now is a good moment to be the bond-buyer. The 10-yr Treasury is at 5.22% (2026-10-08). A 7-year-duration intermediate ladder bought at that yield locks in roughly 5.2% nominal (~1.5–2% real) for the next decade. In 2022 the same ladder was losing ~18%. That's the lock-in benefit: same future rate shock, much less damage, because the carry is locked. And the cash bucket — 5–10% in T-bills — earns ~4% right now, so the 2022-style protection pays you to hold it.

Bottom line

"Age = bond %" is a clean rule and a wrong one. At every age it's tested, it lands at or below 60/40, and at the ages where it matters (65+) it cuts your median 25–38% with no floor benefit to show for it. What the data supports is a tent, not a ladder: 30–50% bonds at retirement, peaking near the date if you're within 5 years, tapering down after the red zone, plus a 5–10% cash/T-bill bucket that's the actual 2022 insurance — and, right now, at a 5.2% 10-yr yield, the bonds you're buying are paying you to hold them.

Honest caveats

This is 47 years of U.S. data (1979–2025) with 10-yr Treasuries as the "bond" — a pure-duration measure, not an aggregate bond fund (the Agg is what a retail bond fund actually holds; it was -13.1% in 2022 vs. -17.8% for the 10-yr). Constant allocation, no withdrawals — so this measures growth distribution, not the sequence-of-returns risk of actually spending down. 2026 YTD figures are estimates from FRED daily, not actuals. It's a strong argument, not a proof: the next 20 years are not the last 47.

How I got the numbers

Annual total returns: Damodaran's historical dataset (Stern/NYU, 1928–2024, Jan 2026 update) for S&P 500 TR, 10-yr T-bond TR, 3-mo T-bill. Monte Carlo: 200,000 bootstrap paths per allocation, 20-yr horizon, annual resampling with replacement from 47 annual pairs, seed 42, constant allocation, no withdrawals. Rolling 20-yr worst windows: full 98-year Damodaran sample. 2022 bond/60/40 numbers: The Wealth Advisor, Morningstar, Reuters. 2026 YTD: my estimate from FRED daily (S&P price + dividend; 10-yr bond = -7.5-yr duration × ΔY + avg carry). Practitioner framework: Kitces / Pfau "bond tent" and rising equity glidepath, Morningstar 2024 bucket-strategy study, Estrada 2019 on the behavioral (not financial) case for buckets.
