Is Now a Good Time to Buy Treasuries and TIPS?

October 10, 2026 · all posts
In plain English: yes, with a specific catch

The one-line answer: yes, this is a historically good time to lock in real yield — if you use the vehicle that locks it in. The number that matters is the green line, not the blue one. A nominal 10-year’s 5.22% sounds like a lot, but after the market-implied 2.33% of inflation it’s the same 2.89% real that a 10-year TIPS pays — and the TIPS gets that 2.89% no matter what inflation does, while the nominal bond gets it only if CPI stays below 2.33%. Real yields of ~2.9% at the 10-year sit near the top of the range since 2010, so you’re buying the lock-in at a historically rich price. If you want the maximum, the 30-year TIPS locks in ~3.34% real — the highest real yield on the curve.

How to use it, concretely: split the money by what you’d actually do with it. The cash-like portion — the stuff you don’t want to lock into a price swing — goes into a 1-year T-bill ladder at 4.62%, zero price risk, redeployed annually. The core long-money goes into 10-year TIPS (2.89% real locked), or 30-year TIPS if you’re genuinely holding a decade or more and can stomach a ~15% price swing if rates rise. Nominal Treasuries make sense only if you actually believe the Fed’s own base case — inflation falling to 2.3% by 2027 — because then the nominal’s higher coupon is pure win. VBTLX is the hands-off alternative (one fund, ~4.5–4.6%, duration 5.7) and I-Bonds are the $10k/year garnish: 4.26% composite, state-tax-exempt, real return floored at zero, but capped and locked so they can’t be a core position.

The catch — and it cuts both directions. Everyone’s instinct is “buy long and ride it down,” but that framing assumes rates can only fall. Right now they can’t be assumed to do either: the Fed just hiked (target is 3.75–4.00% after Sept 16), and the median Fed participant still expects the 2026 dot at 4.1% — that’s one more hike, then a long hold. The market prices only ~16–18% odds of a hike at the very next meeting (Oct 28), but the median dot is the honest prior. So the live duration risk is to the upside: if rates rise 100bp, a 10-year’s price drops ~8.5% — including in TIPS, because locking in the real yield does not lock in the price. That’s the trade you’re making: real return is fixed, price is not. The other direction: this CPI spike is energy-led (headline 3.4%, core 2.4% and falling, energy +16.3%). If the energy shock rolls off and CPI heads to target, rates fall, and then nominal Treasuries and VBTLX’s duration pay off with capital gains — the TIPS holder locks in 2.89% real and simply doesn’t get the extra pop. Neither direction is free: you’re choosing which bet you can lose, and TIPS are the only vehicle on the list where the answer is “neither, as far as the real return goes.”
