What Percent in Bonds Should You Retire With? One Table, Two Numbers In, One Number Out

Short version: the honest answer depends on exactly two things — how old you are today, and the age you plan to retire at. That table is below: two numbers in, one bond % out. The instinct from Part 1 is real — proximity to retirement is what dominates your current age — and the data turns that instinct into cells.

Part 2 — read Part 1 first

This is the follow-up to Part 1: "Does 'Age = Bond %' Still Hold?" — the post that tested the classic "your age in bonds" rule against 47 years of data (1979–2025), found it fails at every age, and landed on a 30–50% band with a 55% "bond-tent" peak for people within about five years of retirement. Part 1 answered "what is the band?" — this post answers "which cell of the table am I in?" Everything below is built on the same 47 annual (S&P 500 TR, 10-yr T-bond TR) pairs, the same 200,000-path Monte Carlo, the same seed. No new data was pulled for this post.

How to read it

  • Columns set the long-run band, by your post-retirement runway — the years you expect to live after the retirement date, anchored at life expectancy 85. Retire at 55 → 30-yr runway → 30%; 60 → 25-yr → 35%; 65 → 20-yr → 40%; 70 → 15-yr → 45%; 75 → 10-yr → 50%. Longer runway → lower bond %; shorter runway → higher bond %.
  • The bold diagonal is the bond-tent peak, 55% — and it overrides the band whenever your planned retirement is within ~5 years. That's the "tent" from Part 1: bonds peak in the ~5 years before you retire, then taper back toward the band after. Read across that bold band and you'll notice the pattern: same years-to-retirement → same answer. Proximity to retirement dominates your current age.
  • The blanks (—) are where retirement age ≤ age today — you're already at or past your planned date.

How the table was built

Two layers, both anchored to Part 1's framework. Layer 1 — the long-run band (the columns): Part 1's evidence-based result is 30–50% bonds at retirement for a ~20-year retirement, so the 20-year anchor (retire at 65) sits at the band's midpoint, 40%. The same Monte Carlo as Part 1 (47 annual S&P TR / 10-yr T-bond pairs, 1979–2025, 200,000 bootstrap paths, seed 42, constant allocation, no withdrawals) shows that within that band the worst-5% floor is essentially flat while the median falls as bonds rise — so the band steps up only to buy stability as your post-retirement runway shortens, and the 20-yr cell sits at 40%. Layer 2 — the bond-tent peak (the rows): a sequence-risk check (first 3 years = the actual 2000–02 dot-com burst, then horizon-length bootstrap) is what justifies the 55% peak — at the 20-yr anchor it lifts the worst-5% floor ~18% (2.16 → 2.56 dollars per dollar) at only ~3% cost to the median (4.79 → 4.66), and it turns a 2000–02-style start from a loss (0.82×) into a net-positive first three years (1.01×). That trade is the tent, and it's why the within-5-years cells read 55% instead of the band.

Assumptions — every one of them

1. Data window / source: 47 annual total-return pairs, 1979–2025, US-only — S&P 500 (total return, incl. dividends) + 10-yr US Treasury (total return) — from the Part 1 research (Damodaran historical returns + FRED). No year before 1979 and nothing after 2025 is in the sample.
2. Bond asset = 10-yr US Treasury total return — a pure-duration measure, NOT the Bloomberg Aggregate or a retail bond fund. In 2022 the 10-yr T-bond was −17.83%; a retail Agg fund was −13.1%. The table's bond % is a portfolio weight against this asset, not against an Agg fund.
3. Constant allocation, no withdrawals. The growth Monte Carlo measures growth/distribution only, not drawdowns, income, or sequence-of-withdrawals. The separate sequence-risk table (first 3 years = actual 2000–02) is the supplement for drawdown/income risk.
4. Horizon at retirement = 85 − retirement age (planning life expectancy). 85 is a fixed planning anchor, not a guarantee; living materially longer or shorter than 85 changes the optimal horizon and hence the cell.
5. 2026 YTD figures are estimates, not actuals (S&P price + ~1.3% dividend; 10-yr bond = duration 7.5 × ΔY + average carry; T-bill ≈ avg Fed funds). The 2026 YTD row is not in the bootstrap sample — the Monte Carlo uses only the 47 full years 1979–2025.
6. No taxes, no fees/expenses, no income/spending modeling, no Social Security or pension offset — pure portfolio growth.
7. The band is evidence-based, not a proof or a guarantee. The Monte Carlo bootstraps 47 historical years, so it reflects the last ~47 years' joint stock/bond distribution, not all possible futures. 30–50% / 50–60% are ranges the data + practitioner literature support, not exact optima.
8. The % shown is for the retirement DATE. The actual glidepath rises to the tent peak in the ~5 years before the date and tapers back toward the band in the years after. The within-5-yr cells show the peak (55%); the other cells show the steady-state band target.
9. "Within ~5 years" trigger = 0 < retirement age − current age ≤ 5. Cells with retirement age ≤ current age are blank (not applicable).
10. Single diversified portfolio held to and beyond the retirement date; the table does not model rebalancing costs, tax-lot management, or the separate 5–10% cash/T-bill income bucket, which is additive to (not a substitute for) the bond allocation — that cash bucket is what actually protected 2022-style scenarios in the Part 1 data.
11. Single country, single duration: S&P 500 + 10-yr US T-bond. International diversification, other durations/maturities, TIPS, and real yields are out of scope.
12. The ~20-yr "retirement" framing and 85 life-expectancy anchor assume a standard retiree who lives to 85; the Part 1 30–50% band is stated for that ~20-yr case, and this matrix extrapolates it to 30-yr (retire 55) and 10-yr (retire 75) runways.

What this means for you

Plain English: your current age mostly tells you when you're getting to the table, not what the table says. If you're more than five years from your planned retirement, your bond % at the date is set by how long you expect to live after it — retire at 65 and plan for 40% bonds, retire at 70 and plan for 45%, retire at 55 and plan for 30% — and it barely changes what you do today except keep your stocks working. If you're within about five years, stop looking at the band: go to 55% bonds at the date (rising into it now, tapering back after), because that's where the data says a 2000–02-style start stops being a loss. And either way, the table's bond % is not a replacement for the 5–10% cash/T-bill bucket Part 1 talks about — that bucket is additive, and it's the piece that actually protected 2022-style scenarios.

How I got the numbers

Every number in this post comes from the Part 1 research dataset and Monte Carlo — no new data pulls. Annual total returns: Damodaran's historical dataset (Stern/NYU, 1928–2024, Jan 2026 update) for S&P 500 TR and 10-yr T-bond TR. Growth Monte Carlo: 200,000 bootstrap paths, horizon = 85 − retirement age, annual resampling with replacement from the 47 annual pairs (1979–2025), seed 42, constant allocation, no withdrawals; validated against Part 1's own MC (reproduces its 20-yr results within 0.02 at every bond %). Sequence-risk check: first 3 years = actual 2000–02, then horizon-length bootstrap, at 30% / 55% / 60% bonds. Long-run band: Part 1's 30–50% evidence-based band, 20-yr anchor at midpoint 40%, stepped by post-retirement horizon. Practitioner framework: Kitces / Pfau "bond tent" and rising equity glidepath. 2026 YTD figures are estimates, not actuals, and are not in the bootstrap sample.
